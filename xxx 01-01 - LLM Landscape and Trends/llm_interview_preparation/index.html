    LLM Interview Preparation¶

    Originally by Hao Hoang, May 2025

    Opening Briefing¶

    The standard framing of LLM knowledge treats architecture, training, and deployment as separate disciplines to be studied in isolation. That framing is wrong. The defining characteristic of modern large language models is that every design decision propagates causally through the entire stack — tokenization choices constrain attention behavior, attention complexity dictates viable training regimes, and training regimes determine what deployment trade-offs are even available. Mastering LLM interview questions means internalizing these causal chains, not memorizing fifty independent facts.

    Consider the numbers that actually govern the field. Scaled dot-product attention carries O(n^2) complexity in sequence length, which is the single architectural constraint that explains why context windows remained small for years, why sparse attention is an active research front, and why retrieval-augmented generation exists as a deployment pattern rather than an academic curiosity. Meanwhile, Mixture of Experts architectures activate roughly 10% of their parameters per forward pass, which means a model with hundreds of billions of parameters can run at a fraction of the expected compute cost — but only if the gating mechanism routes inputs correctly, introducing a new failure mode that pure dense architectures never faced. QLoRA pushes a 70-billion-parameter model onto a single GPU by combining low-rank adaptation with 4-bit quantization, collapsing what was once a cluster-scale fine-tuning problem into commodity hardware. These are not trivia; they are the pressure points where theoretical understanding meets engineering reality.

    What these observations share is a second-order consequence: the boundary between "understanding LLMs" and "deploying LLMs" has dissolved. Catastrophic forgetting is not just a training nuisance — it dictates whether parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques like LoRA are viable in production. The autoregressive-versus-masked model split is not a taxonomy exercise — it determines whether your system can generate text, classify it, or both, and whether beam search or nucleus sampling is the appropriate decoding strategy downstream.

    This document operates on the claim that LLM competence is architectural reasoning under constraints, not vocabulary recall. The fifty questions that follow are organized to surface these causal dependencies — from tokenization and attention mechanics through training dynamics, adaptation strategies, and deployment trade-offs — so that each answer reinforces the connective tissue between them rather than standing alone.

    Fundamentals & Architecture¶

    1. Tokenization -- Breaking text into subwords/characters (e.g., "artificial" -> "art" + "ific" + "ial"). LLMs process numerical token representations, not raw text. Enables handling of rare/unknown words. Methods: BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding), WordPiece, SentencePiece.

    2. Attention mechanism -- Weighs importance of different tokens via query, key, value vectors. Computes similarity scores (dot products) to focus on relevant tokens. E.g., linking "mouse" to "chased" in context.

    3. Context window -- Number of tokens an LLM can process at once (its "memory"). Larger window (e.g., 32k tokens) improves coherence but increases computational cost. Balancing size vs. efficiency is critical.

    7. Masked language modeling (MLM) -- Hides random tokens, trains model to predict them from context. Used in BERT. Fosters bidirectional understanding. Equips models for sentiment analysis, QA.

    8. Sequence-to-sequence (Seq2Seq) -- Encoder processes input, decoder generates output (possibly different length). Applications: machine translation, summarization, chatbots.

    9. Autoregressive vs. masked models -- Autoregressive (GPT): predict next token sequentially, excel at generation. Masked (BERT): predict masked tokens from bidirectional context, excel at understanding/classification.

    10. Embeddings -- Dense vectors representing tokens in continuous space, capturing semantic/syntactic properties. Initialized randomly or from pretrained models (GloVe), then fine-tuned during training.

    11. Next sentence prediction (NSP) -- BERT pretraining task: classify if two sentences are consecutive or random. Improves coherence for dialogue and summarization.

    17. Transformers vs. traditional Seq2Seq -- Transformers improve via: - Self-attention enables parallel processing (unlike sequential RNNs) - Long-range dependencies captured by attention - Positional encodings preserve sequence order

    21. Positional encodings -- Add sequence order info to transformer inputs (sinusoidal functions or learned vectors). Necessary because self-attention lacks inherent order awareness.

    22. Multi-head attention -- Splits Q, K, V into multiple subspaces, letting model focus on different aspects simultaneously (syntax in one head, semantics in another). Captures complex patterns.

    46. Encoders vs. decoders -- Encoders process input into abstract representations. Decoders generate output using encoder output + prior tokens. In translation: encoder understands source, decoder produces target language.

    49. What defines an LLM? -- AI systems trained on vast text corpora with billions of parameters. Excel at translation, summarization, QA. Leverage contextual learning.

    Decoding & Generation¶

    5. Beam search vs. greedy decoding -- Greedy: picks most probable token at each step. Beam search: keeps top k candidates (beams), e.g., k=5. Produces more coherent/diverse outputs, especially for translation and dialogue.

    6. Temperature -- Controls randomness of token selection. Low (0.3): high-probability tokens, predictable. High (1.5): flatter distribution, more diverse. 0.8: common balance for creativity + coherence.

    12. Top-k vs. top-p sampling -- Top-k: sample from k most probable tokens (e.g., k=20). Top-p (nucleus): sample from smallest set whose cumulative probability exceeds p (e.g., 0.95). Top-p is more adaptive to context, offers more flexibility.

    42. Adaptive Softmax -- Groups words by frequency, reducing computation for rare words. Speeds up training/inference for large vocabularies in resource-limited settings.

    Training & Optimization¶

    13. Prompt engineering -- Designing inputs to elicit desired LLM responses. Clear prompts (e.g., "Summarize in 100 words") outperform vague ones. Critical for zero-shot and few-shot settings.

    14. Catastrophic forgetting -- Fine-tuning erases prior knowledge. Mitigations: - Rehearsal: Mix old + new data during training - Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC): Prioritize critical weights - Modular architectures: Task-specific modules to avoid overwriting

    15. Model distillation -- Train smaller "student" to mimic larger "teacher" using soft probabilities. Reduces memory/compute while retaining near-teacher performance. Enables deployment on smartphones.

    18. Overfitting mitigation -- Model memorizes training data, fails to generalize. Fixes: - Regularization (L1/L2 penalties) - Dropout (randomly disable neurons) - Early stopping (halt when validation plateaus)

    48. Hyperparameters -- Preset values (learning rate, batch size) controlling training. High learning rate causes instability. Tuning optimizes LLM efficiency and accuracy.

    Fine-Tuning & Adaptation¶

    4. LoRA vs. QLoRA -- LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation): adds low-rank matrices to model layers, minimal memory overhead. QLoRA: extends LoRA with 4-bit quantization, further reducing memory. QLoRA can fine-tune a 70B-parameter model on a single GPU.

    35. PEFT (Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning) -- Updates only a small subset of parameters, freezing the rest. Techniques like LoRA preserve pretrained knowledge while adapting to new tasks.

    41. Zero-shot learning -- LLMs perform untrained tasks using general knowledge from pretraining. E.g., "Classify this review as positive or negative" without task-specific training data.

    44. Few-shot learning -- Perform tasks with minimal examples. Benefits: reduced data needs, faster adaptation, cost efficiency. Ideal for niche tasks like specialized text classification.

    Math Foundations¶

    23. Softmax in attention -- Normalizes attention scores into probability distribution: softmax(x_i) = e^(x_i) / sum(e^(x_j)). Converts raw similarity scores into weights emphasizing relevant tokens.

    24. Scaled dot-product attention -- Score = Q . K / sqrt(d_k). High scores = relevant tokens. Complexity: O(n^2) for sequence length n, spurring research into sparse attention alternatives.

    25. Cross-entropy loss -- L = -sum(y_i * log(y_hat_i)). Measures divergence between predicted and true token probabilities. Penalizes incorrect predictions, encouraging accurate next-token selection.

    26. Gradient computation for embeddings -- Chain rule during backpropagation: dL/dE = (dL/d_logits) * (d_logits/dE). Adjusts embedding vectors to minimize loss, refining semantic representations.

    27. Jacobian matrix -- Captures partial derivatives of outputs w.r.t. inputs. In transformers, helps compute gradients for multidimensional outputs during backpropagation.

    28. Eigenvalues & eigenvectors -- Eigenvectors = principal directions in data; eigenvalues = their variance. In PCA, selecting high-eigenvalue eigenvectors reduces dimensionality while retaining variance.

    29. KL divergence -- D_KL(P||Q) = sum(P(x) * log(P(x)/Q(x))). Measures difference between two distributions. In LLMs, evaluates how closely predictions match true distributions; guides fine-tuning.

    30. ReLU derivative -- f(x) = max(0, x). Derivative: 1 if x > 0, else 0. Sparsity and non-linearity prevent vanishing gradients. Computationally efficient; widely used in LLMs.

    31. Chain rule in gradient descent -- d/dx f(g(x)) = f'(g(x)) * g'(x). Enables backpropagation to calculate gradients layer by layer across deep architectures.

    32. Full attention formula -- Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QK^T / sqrt(d_k)) * V. Scaled dot product measures token relevance; softmax normalizes scores; result is weighted combination of values.

    Advanced Architectures & Techniques¶

    16. OOV (Out-of-Vocabulary) words -- Handled via subword tokenization (BPE). E.g., "cryptocurrency" -> "crypto" + "currency". Ensures LLMs process rare/new words robustly.

    19. Generative vs. discriminative models -- Generative (GPT): model joint probabilities P(x,y), create new data. Discriminative (BERT): model conditional probabilities P(y|x), classify.

    20. GPT-4 vs. GPT-3 -- GPT-4 improvements: multimodal input (text + images), larger context (25k vs 4,096 tokens), enhanced accuracy through better fine-tuning.

    33. Gemini multimodal training -- Unified architecture combining text + image processing. Advanced attention for cross-modal stability. Self-supervised techniques reduce labeled data needs.

    34. Foundation model types -- Language (BERT, GPT-4), Vision (ResNet), Generative (DALL-E), Multimodal (CLIP). All leverage broad pretraining for diverse applications.

    36. RAG steps -- 1. Retrieval: Fetch relevant docs via query embeddings. 2. Ranking: Sort by relevance. 3. Generation: Use retrieved context to generate accurate responses. Enhances factual accuracy.

    37. Mixture of Experts (MoE) -- Gating function activates specific expert sub-networks per input. Only ~10% of parameters used per query. Enables billion-parameter models to operate efficiently.

    38. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting -- Guides LLMs to solve problems step-by-step, mimicking human reasoning. Breaks complex calculations into logical steps. Improves accuracy on math, logic, multi-step queries.

    39. Discriminative vs. generative AI -- Discriminative: predicts labels from features (conditional probabilities). Generative: creates new data by modeling joint probabilities. GPT = generative; sentiment classifiers = discriminative.

    40. Knowledge graph integration -- Structured factual data enhances LLMs by: reducing hallucinations (verify facts against graph), improving reasoning (entity relationships), enhancing context.

    43. Vanishing gradient solutions in transformers -- Self-attention (avoids sequential dependencies), residual connections (direct gradient flow), layer normalization (stabilizes updates).

    Deployment & Ethics¶

    45. Fixing biased/incorrect outputs -- 1. Analyze bias sources in data/prompts. 2. Use balanced datasets + debiasing techniques. 3. Fine-tune with curated data or adversarial methods.

    47. LLMs vs. statistical language models -- LLMs use transformers, massive datasets, unsupervised pretraining. Statistical models (N-grams) use simpler supervised methods. LLMs handle long-range dependencies and contextual embeddings but require significant compute.

    50. Deployment challenges -- - Resource intensity: High computational demands - Bias: Risk of perpetuating training data biases - Interpretability: Complex models hard to explain - Privacy: Potential data security concerns
