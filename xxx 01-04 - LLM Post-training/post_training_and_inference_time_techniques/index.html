    Post-Training & Inference-Time Techniques¶

    See also: LLM Landscape & Trends | Inference Engineering | RL Principles & Frontiers | AI Engineering Practices

    Opening Briefing¶

    The most consequential shift in LLM capability over the past two years has not come from scaling pretraining compute — it has come from what happens after pretraining ends and what happens at inference time. These two phases, post-training and inference-time computation, are now the primary levers for turning a base model into a useful system, yet they operate under fundamentally different economics. Post-training techniques like RLHF, DPO, and constitutional AI impose a fixed cost that amortizes across every future inference call, while inference-time techniques like chain-of-thought prompting, self-consistency, and best-of-N sampling impose a per-call cost that scales with usage. The strategic question for any deployment is not which family of techniques to use, but where on this fixed-vs-marginal cost curve to invest.

    Three observations make this concrete. First, reinforcement learning from human feedback compressed the gap between a 1.3B-parameter model and a 175B-parameter model — InstructGPT was preferred over GPT-3 despite being 135x smaller — demonstrating that post-training can substitute for pretraining scale at dramatically lower cost. Second, inference-time compute scaling shows a different kind of elasticity: OpenAI's o-series and DeepSeek-R1 achieve frontier reasoning performance not through larger weights but through spending more tokens at generation time, with verifiable rewards in code and math enabling RL training that teaches models when to think longer. The two approaches are now converging — GRPO's two-phase dynamic shows that RL first corrects procedural errors (low-hanging fruit), then develops strategic planning capabilities, meaning the same post-training run produces both efficiency gains and qualitatively new behaviors. Third, prompting strategies like analogical prompting and least-to-most decomposition can recover 15-30% of the reasoning gap between a base model and a fine-tuned one, at zero training cost — but only when the task structure matches the technique's inductive bias, which is why a taxonomy of when each technique works matters more than knowing they exist.

    The implication is that capability is no longer a property of the model alone — it is a property of the model-technique stack. A well-chosen inference-time strategy on a mid-tier model routinely outperforms a frontier model with naive prompting. This reframes the engineering question from "which model do I use" to "which combination of post-training investment and inference-time computation minimizes total cost per unit of task performance." This document maps both sides of that equation: the post-training methods that shape what a model knows and prefers, and the inference-time techniques that shape how it reasons on any given input.

    Post-Training Methods¶

    Post-training transforms a base model (which predicts tokens) into a system that follows instructions, refuses harmful requests, and exhibits desired behaviors. The methods fall into three tiers of increasing complexity and capability.

    Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)¶

    The simplest post-training step: train on curated (prompt, ideal-response) pairs. SFT teaches format, tone, and task-following but cannot express preferences between responses — it only shows what good looks like, not why one answer is better than another. Overfitting is the primary risk; the model memorizes surface patterns rather than learning generalizable instruction-following. The practical decision hierarchy is: start with prompting, add RAG if context is needed, fine-tune only when prompting proves insufficient, then distill for cost.

    Parameter-efficient variants reduce the cost of SFT dramatically: - LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) — injects small trainable matrices into frozen layers; ~0.1-1% of parameters updated - QLoRA — combines 4-bit quantization with LoRA, enabling fine-tuning of 65B+ models on a single consumer GPU - PEFT — umbrella term for LoRA, prefix-tuning, adapters, and similar lightweight methods

    Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)¶

    The breakthrough that made ChatGPT possible. RLHF adds a preference signal that SFT cannot express: given two responses to the same prompt, which does a human prefer? The pipeline has three stages:

    1. Reward model training — A classifier learns to predict human preferences from comparison data. This is an inverse RL problem: inferring the reward function from expert behavior (Maximum Entropy IRL provides the theoretical grounding).
    2. Policy optimization — The language model is fine-tuned via PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) to maximize the learned reward while staying close to the SFT baseline (KL penalty prevents reward hacking).
    3. Iterative refinement — New generations are sampled, re-ranked, and fed back into training.

    RLHF reports ~85% human preference win rates over SFT-only models. InstructGPT (1.3B parameters, RLHF-trained) was preferred over the 175B GPT-3 — arguably the most impactful real-world RL application to date, with millions of users interacting with RLHF-tuned models daily.

    Failure modes: Reward hacking (model finds outputs that score high on the reward model but are actually low quality), mode collapse (diversity loss under aggressive optimization), and reward model staleness (preferences shift but the reward model is frozen).

    Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)¶

    DPO eliminates the reward model entirely. Instead of training a separate classifier and then running RL against it, DPO directly optimizes the language model on preference pairs using a closed-form loss function. This collapses the three-stage RLHF pipeline into a single training step.

    DPO vs RLHF trade-offs: - DPO is simpler, cheaper, and more stable to train - RLHF with a reward model provides a reusable scoring signal (useful for best-of-N at inference time and ongoing evaluation) - DPO may underperform RLHF on tasks where the reward landscape is complex and requires online exploration - In practice, many teams start with DPO and upgrade to RLHF only when DPO plateaus

    Constitutional AI (CAI)¶

    Anthropic's approach to alignment without human preference labels for harmful content. The model critiques its own outputs against a set of written principles (the "constitution"), then is trained on its own revisions via RLHF. This enables scaling safety alignment without requiring humans to read and rank harmful outputs — a significant practical advantage as models become more capable.

    Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)¶

    DeepSeek's alternative to PPO that groups multiple sampled responses and computes relative advantages within each group, eliminating the need for a separate value network. GRPO's training exhibits a distinctive two-phase dynamic: the first phase focuses on procedural error correction (fixing calculation mistakes, formatting issues — low-hanging fruit), while the second phase develops strategic planning capabilities (choosing better solution approaches, backtracking when stuck). This means a single GRPO run produces both efficiency gains and qualitatively new reasoning behaviors.

    Cutting-Edge Post-Training (2026)¶

    • RL + Synthetic Data (Databricks KARL, March 2026) — Generate synthetic task data, apply efficient large-batch off-policy RL (OAPL), regenerate harder data with the improved model, iterate. KARL beats Claude 4.6 and GPT-5.2 on enterprise knowledge tasks at ~33% lower cost and ~47% lower latency. The key insight: RL learns to search more efficiently — stopping earlier and issuing fewer wasted queries.
    • Hypernetwork-generated LoRA (Sakana AI Doc-to-LoRA) — A hypernetwork generates LoRA adapters from documents or task descriptions in a single forward pass, enabling memory and skill updates at runtime without gradient-based fine-tuning. Enables continual learning without catastrophic forgetting.
    • Pretraining replay during fine-tuning — Mixing generic pretraining data into the fine-tuning dataset reduces catastrophic forgetting and improves domain performance when fine-tuning data is scarce (Kotha & Liang, March 2026).
    • Verifiable rewards as RL signal — For tasks with checkable outputs (code execution, math proofs, logical consistency, structured formats), the verification function itself serves as the reward model, eliminating the need for learned reward models and their associated failure modes. This is the structural breakthrough enabling models like DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI's o-series to achieve frontier reasoning.

    Inference-Time Techniques¶

    Inference-time techniques improve model outputs without any training. They work by structuring how the model reasons about a problem, generating multiple candidates, or using the model to verify its own work. The cost is measured in additional tokens generated (and therefore additional latency and API spend), not in training compute.

    Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting¶

    The foundational inference-time reasoning technique. Instead of asking for a direct answer, prompt the model to show its reasoning steps before concluding. CoT works because transformer attention over intermediate tokens effectively extends the model's "working memory" — each reasoning step becomes context for the next.

    Three variants with different requirements:

    • Few-Shot CoT — Include 2-5 examples of step-by-step reasoning in the prompt. The demonstrations teach the model the expected reasoning format. Most reliable but costs prompt tokens.
    • Instruction CoT — Add "Let's think step by step" or "Show your reasoning" to the prompt. Zero examples needed but less controlled — the model decides what "reasoning" looks like.
    • Zero-Shot CoT — The model generates reasoning spontaneously when the task difficulty triggers it (more common in instruction-tuned models). Least reliable but zero prompt overhead.

    Key: CoT's effectiveness scales with task complexity. On simple factual recall, it adds latency without improving accuracy. On multi-step reasoning (math, logic, planning), it can close 20-40% of the gap between a weaker and stronger model.

    Analogical Prompting¶

    Rather than providing fixed examples, the model generates its own relevant examples before solving the problem. The prompt instructs: "Think of similar problems you've seen and how they were solved, then apply that approach here." This works because the model's pretraining data contains solutions to analogous problems — analogical prompting surfaces relevant patterns that fixed few-shot examples might miss.

    When it outperforms standard CoT: Tasks where the right reasoning strategy is non-obvious and where the model's training distribution contains relevant analogies (mathematical reasoning, code debugging, scientific problem-solving). Less effective when the task is novel enough that no good analogies exist in the training data.

    Least-to-Most Prompting¶

    A decomposition strategy: break a complex problem into simpler sub-problems, solve them in order from easiest to hardest, and use each sub-solution as context for the next. Two stages:

    1. Decomposition — "What sub-problems do I need to solve first?"
    2. Sequential solving — Solve sub-problem 1, feed the answer into sub-problem 2, and so on.

    This is particularly effective for tasks with compositional structure — multi-step math, multi-hop question answering, code generation with dependencies. The technique explicitly prevents the model from attempting to solve everything at once, which is where most reasoning failures occur.

    Self-Consistency¶

    Instead of taking the model's first answer, sample N independent reasoning chains (using temperature > 0) and take the majority vote. The insight: correct reasoning paths tend to converge on the same answer, while incorrect paths diverge. Self-consistency converts the model's stochastic generation into a more reliable signal through statistical aggregation.

    Practical parameters: - N = 5-20 samples is typical; diminishing returns beyond ~40 - Works best when the task has a verifiable correct answer (math, logic, factual questions) - Cost scales linearly with N — a 10-sample self-consistency run costs 10x a single generation - Can be combined with CoT: each sample uses chain-of-thought, then vote on final answers

    Universal Self-Consistency (USC)¶

    An extension of self-consistency for tasks where answers cannot be directly compared or majority-voted (open-ended generation, summaries, explanations). Instead of voting, the model itself evaluates which of the N generated responses is most consistent with the others. This adds a meta-reasoning step: "Given these N responses, which one best represents the consensus?"

    USC trades the simplicity of majority voting for applicability to a much wider range of tasks, at the cost of an additional LLM call for the consistency evaluation.

    Self-Debugging / Self-Correction¶

    The model generates an output, evaluates it against criteria (explicit or implicit), and revises if the evaluation fails. For code, this is literal debugging: run the code, observe the error, fix it. For text, it is self-critique: "Does this answer actually address the question? Is the reasoning valid?"

    Key limitation: Models are not reliable self-critics by default. Self-correction works best when: - The evaluation criteria are concrete and verifiable (code runs or doesn't, math checks out or doesn't) - External feedback is available (compiler errors, test results, API responses) - The model is explicitly prompted with evaluation rubrics

    Without grounded feedback, self-correction can degrade outputs — the model "corrects" correct answers into incorrect ones based on spurious self-criticism.

    Feedback-Enhanced Reasoning¶

    A generalization of self-debugging: any external signal (tool output, retrieval results, human feedback, environment observations) is fed back into the model's reasoning loop. This transforms single-pass generation into an iterative refinement process.

    Instantiations in practice: - ReAct (Reasoning + Acting) — The model alternates between reasoning steps and tool calls, using tool outputs to inform next reasoning steps - Reflexion — The model generates, evaluates, reflects on failures, and retries with accumulated lessons - Verifier-guided generation — A separate model or program scores intermediate outputs, and the generator conditions on these scores

    Best-of-N with Verifier¶

    Generate N candidate responses and use a verifier (reward model, code executor, or separate LLM) to select the best one. This separates generation from evaluation, allowing each to be optimized independently.

    This is the bridge between post-training and inference-time techniques: the reward model trained during RLHF can be repurposed at inference time as a verifier for best-of-N selection, getting additional value from the post-training investment without further training.

    Inference-Time Compute Scaling¶

    The frontier paradigm (OpenAI o-series, DeepSeek-R1, Gemini 2.5 thinking): models that dynamically allocate more computation to harder problems. Rather than a fixed forward pass, the model generates extended reasoning chains, explores multiple solution branches, backtracks from dead ends, and verifies intermediate steps — all within a single inference call.

    Key mechanisms: - Elastic thinking budgets — GPT-5 fast vs thinking router, Gemini 2.5 controllable thinking budget, Claude Sonnet 4.6 effort parameter, gpt-oss reasoning levels (low/medium/high) - Branch exploration — The model explores multiple reasoning paths internally, pruning unpromising ones - Self-verification — Intermediate conclusions are checked before proceeding - Revision loops — The model rewrites earlier reasoning when later steps reveal errors

    Key: Inference-time compute scaling turns token budget into a direct lever for capability. A model spending 10x more tokens on reasoning can match or exceed a model 10x larger with minimal reasoning. This is why the cost-performance frontier is now a function of (model size × inference compute), not model size alone.

    Technique Selection Guide¶

    The choice of technique depends on three factors: task structure, cost tolerance, and whether training compute is available.

    Scenario Recommended Techniques Why
    Simple factual/retrieval tasks Direct prompting, RAG CoT adds latency without accuracy gains
    Multi-step reasoning (math, logic) CoT + Self-Consistency Decomposition + statistical aggregation covers most failures
    Complex compositional tasks Least-to-Most + CoT Explicit decomposition prevents the model from getting lost
    Tasks with verifiable outputs (code) Self-Debugging + Verifier External feedback grounds the correction loop
    Novel/unfamiliar problem types Analogical Prompting Surfaces relevant training-data analogies
    Open-ended generation quality USC + Best-of-N No single "correct" answer to vote on; need meta-evaluation
    Maximum reliability, cost secondary Best-of-N + Self-Consistency + Verifier Multiple redundant reliability layers
    Training budget available RLHF/DPO + inference-time verification Amortize alignment cost, spend per-call only on hard inputs

    The Fixed vs. Marginal Cost Framework¶

    The strategic insight connecting post-training and inference-time techniques:

    • Post-training (SFT, RLHF, DPO) imposes a fixed cost — one training run that improves every subsequent inference call. The amortized cost per call approaches zero at scale.
    • Inference-time techniques (CoT, self-consistency, best-of-N) impose a marginal cost — each call is more expensive, and the cost never amortizes.
    • Optimal strategy depends on volume: high-volume deployments should invest heavily in post-training to minimize per-call overhead; low-volume or diverse-task deployments should rely on inference-time techniques to avoid training costs that won't amortize.
    • Hybrid approach (the emerging standard): post-train for baseline quality and safety, then layer inference-time techniques selectively on hard inputs. Elastic thinking budgets (o-series, Gemini 2.5) automate this — the model itself decides when to spend more inference compute.
