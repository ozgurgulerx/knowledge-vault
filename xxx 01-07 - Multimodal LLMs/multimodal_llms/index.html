    Multimodal LLMs: Architectures & Capabilities¶

    See also: LLM Architectures | Phi-4-Reasoning-Vision | Multimodal & Real-Time | AI Security

    Opening Briefing¶

    The phrase "multimodal model" has become one of the most overloaded terms in AI. For most of 2023, it meant a vision encoder duct-taped to a language model through a linear projection layer — a bolt-on arrangement where the language backbone did the real work and the image signal arrived as a second-class citizen, pre-digested into a handful of embedding vectors. That era is ending. The architectural choices that define today's frontier multimodal systems — early fusion, mid-fusion, and native multimodal training — are not incremental refinements of the bolt-on paradigm. They represent fundamentally different bets on where cross-modal understanding should live in the computation graph, and each bet carries distinct trade-offs in capability, cost, and modularity.

    The numbers make the divergence concrete. Gemini 2.5 Pro processes over one million tokens of interleaved text, image, video, and audio, trained natively on all modalities from the start — no adapters, no projection layers, no post-hoc alignment. Meta's Llama 4 Scout and Maverick mix visual tokens directly into the text stream from the first transformer layer, achieving early fusion at 10B-active-parameter scale with mixture-of-experts routing. Meanwhile, Microsoft's Phi-4-reasoning-vision-15B takes the pragmatic mid-fusion path: a frozen SigLIP-2 vision encoder feeds through an MLP projector into a 14B reasoning backbone that was already strong at chain-of-thought mathematics. The ablation studies from Phi-4 are instructive — 3,600 visual tokens (roughly 720p equivalent) turned out to be the sweet spot where adding more tokens stopped improving benchmark scores and started degrading inference speed. Architecture matters, but so does the token budget.

    The second-order consequence of these architectural splits is that multimodal capability is no longer a single axis. A model can be excellent at visual question answering (perception) and poor at spatial reasoning over diagrams (cross-modal inference), or vice versa. Native multimodal training tends to produce the most uniform capability profile across tasks, but it demands training infrastructure that only a handful of labs can afford. Mid-fusion architectures let smaller teams bootstrap multimodal capability on top of strong text-only checkpoints — which is why the open-source ecosystem is converging on that pattern, from Phi-4-reasoning-vision to Qwen3-VL.

    The practical implication for builders is that model selection for multimodal tasks now requires understanding fusion strategy, not just headline benchmark numbers. A mid-fusion model with a strong reasoning backbone may outperform a natively multimodal model on structured visual reasoning, while underperforming on free-form video understanding. This document maps the architectural landscape, the encoder choices, the reasoning-mode innovations, and the safety surfaces that expand the moment a model starts accepting pixels alongside text.

    Fusion Strategies¶

    The way visual and textual information combine inside a model is the single most consequential architectural decision in multimodal LLM design. Four broad strategies have emerged, each with a distinct capability-cost profile.

    Early Fusion¶

    Early fusion mixes visual tokens with text tokens from the very first transformer layer. The model sees no distinction between modalities at the attention level — image patches and text subwords compete for the same attention heads from layer one onward.

    • Representative model: Llama 4 (Scout 17B-active/109B-total, Maverick 17B-active/400B-total)
    • How it works: Images are patchified and projected into the same embedding space as text tokens before entering the transformer stack. All subsequent layers operate on a unified token sequence.
    • Strengths: Deepest possible cross-modal reasoning. The model can learn fine-grained correspondences between visual regions and textual concepts at every layer of abstraction.
    • Weaknesses: Extremely expensive to train — you cannot start from a text-only checkpoint; the model must be trained (or at minimum heavily fine-tuned) on interleaved multimodal data from scratch. Harder to iterate on vision components independently.
    • Key results: Llama 4 Scout achieves competitive image understanding benchmarks while routing through only 17B active parameters per token via mixture-of-experts.

    Mid-Fusion¶

    Mid-fusion keeps a separate vision encoder and connects it to the language model through a learned projection — typically an MLP or cross-attention adapter — that maps visual representations into the LLM's embedding space.

    • Representative model: Phi-4-reasoning-vision-15B
    • How it works: A frozen or lightly fine-tuned vision encoder (e.g., SigLIP-2 with NaFlex dynamic resolution) produces a sequence of visual tokens. An MLP projector maps these into the LLM embedding space. The LLM then processes the combined visual + text token sequence.
    • Strengths: Modular — you can upgrade the vision encoder or the language backbone independently. You can bootstrap from a strong text-only reasoning model, preserving its chain-of-thought capabilities. Practical for smaller teams and budgets.
    • Weaknesses: Cross-modal understanding is bounded by the information bottleneck at the projection layer. The language model never sees raw visual features; it sees a lossy summary.
    • Key results: Phi-4-reasoning-vision-15B matches or exceeds much larger models on mathematical diagram reasoning, validating the "strong reasoning backbone + good-enough vision encoder" hypothesis.

    Late Fusion¶

    Late fusion processes each modality through entirely separate encoder stacks, combining outputs only at the final decision layer or through a shallow interaction module.

    • How it works: A vision model and a language model each produce independent representations. These are concatenated, averaged, or combined through a lightweight attention layer at the top of the network.
    • Strengths: Cheapest to build. Each encoder can be optimized independently. Easy to add new modalities.
    • Weaknesses: Weakest cross-modal understanding. The model cannot learn deep correspondences between modalities because the representations only interact at the very end.
    • Status: Largely abandoned for frontier models but still used in retrieval-augmented settings (e.g., CLIP-based retrieval feeding into a separate LLM).

    Native Multimodal Training¶

    Native multimodal training is not a fusion "strategy" in the architectural sense — it is a training regime where the model is trained on interleaved text, image, video, and audio data from the beginning, with all modalities tokenized into a single sequence.

    • Representative model: Gemini 2.5 Pro/Flash
    • How it works: All modalities are tokenized (text → subwords, images → patches or learned tokens, audio → spectral tokens, video → frame sequences) and interleaved in a single context window. The transformer processes the unified sequence end-to-end.
    • Strengths: Most uniform capability profile across tasks. Natural handling of long, multi-modal contexts (hours of video, interleaved documents with figures). Over 1M token context window.
    • Weaknesses: Requires massive training infrastructure and data pipelines that handle all modalities simultaneously. Only feasible for the largest labs.
    • Key results: Gemini 2.5 Pro leads on long-video understanding, interleaved document QA, and tasks requiring joint reasoning across audio and visual streams.

    Google's Gemma 4 brings native multimodal training into the open-weight space under an Apache 2.0 license — a significant shift, since previously only closed models like Gemini offered true native multimodal training. The larger Gemma 4 models (31B dense, 26B MoE) are natively trained on text and vision, while the edge-optimized E2B and E4B variants extend this to trimodal (text + vision + audio) with native audio input via a Conformer architecture. Unlike Gemini's closed ecosystem, Gemma 4 makes native multimodal training accessible for open research and downstream fine-tuning, and the edge models represent one of the first open-weight families to offer edge-deployable trimodal capabilities.

    Vision Encoders¶

    The vision encoder is the component that converts raw pixels into a sequence of tokens the language model can process. Encoder choice determines resolution, token count, and the nature of visual information available downstream.

    SigLIP-2¶

    SigLIP-2 is the successor to Google's SigLIP (Sigmoid Loss for Image-Language Pre-training). It replaces the softmax-based contrastive loss with a sigmoid loss that enables better scaling and more stable training. SigLIP-2 has become the default vision encoder for mid-fusion architectures due to its strong zero-shot transfer and compatibility with dynamic-resolution schemes.

    • Used by: Phi-4-reasoning-vision, several Qwen-VL variants
    • Produces dense visual token sequences that preserve spatial layout

    ViT Variants¶

    Vision Transformer (ViT) variants remain the backbone of most vision encoders. Key variations include:

    • ViT-L/14, ViT-H/14: Standard sizes used across many multimodal models
    • EVA-CLIP ViT: Enhanced training recipe with improved representation quality
    • InternViT: Scaled ViT variants used in InternVL models

    2D Spatial RoPE (Gemma 4)¶

    Gemma 4 takes a different approach to encoding spatial relationships in vision: rather than using a separate patch-based vision encoder like SigLIP-2 or ViT, it applies 2D spatial RoPE (Rotary Position Embeddings) to encode the spatial positions of image regions directly within the transformer. This allows the model to reason about spatial relationships — relative positions, distances, and layout — without a dedicated external vision encoder, and supports native aspect-ratio handling (no forced resize to a fixed resolution). This contrasts with the dominant paradigm of patch-based encoding followed by projection, offering a more tightly integrated approach to vision processing.

    Dynamic Resolution and NaFlex¶

    Fixed-resolution encoders force all images into a single size (e.g., 224x224 or 384x384), losing detail on high-resolution inputs and wasting tokens on low-resolution ones. Dynamic resolution approaches solve this.

    • NaFlex (Native Flexible): Used in SigLIP-2 and adopted by Phi-4-reasoning-vision. Allows the encoder to process images at their native aspect ratio and resolution, packing patches into a variable-length token sequence. Critical for document understanding and GUI grounding where spatial precision matters.
    • Multi-crop: An alternative approach (used by InternVL and others) that splits high-resolution images into multiple fixed-size crops, encodes each independently, and concatenates the token sequences. Simpler but produces more tokens and can break spatial continuity across crop boundaries.

    Token Budget Trade-offs¶

    The number of visual tokens directly impacts both capability and inference cost. The Phi-4-reasoning-vision ablation study provides concrete guidance:

    Token Count Approximate Resolution Benchmark Impact
    1,024 ~384p Acceptable for simple VQA, degrades on fine-grained tasks
    3,600 ~720p Sweet spot — strong across most benchmarks
    6,400+ ~1080p+ Marginal gains on detail-heavy tasks, significant latency increase

    The lesson: more visual tokens help up to a point, but the returns diminish fast. For most practical applications, 3,600 tokens (roughly 720p equivalent) captures the vast majority of visual information a language model can actually use.

    "Think with Images"¶

    The introduction of reasoning models — o1, o3, o4-mini — into the multimodal domain represents a qualitative shift in how models process visual input. These models do not just perceive images; they reason over them using extended chain-of-thought.

    Perception vs. Reasoning over Visual Input¶

    Standard multimodal models operate in a perception mode: they encode the image, extract features, and generate a response in a single forward pass (or a short generation). Reasoning multimodal models like o3 and o4-mini instead:

    1. Encode the image into visual tokens (same as perception models)
    2. Generate an extended internal chain-of-thought that references visual elements — counting objects, tracing spatial relationships, reading text in images, analyzing chart axes
    3. Iterate and self-correct within the reasoning trace before producing a final answer

    This means the model spends significantly more compute at inference time on visual tasks, but achieves substantially better performance on problems that require spatial reasoning, multi-step visual logic, or precise numerical extraction from charts and diagrams.

    What Changes with Reasoning-Mode Vision¶

    • Counting accuracy: Perception models frequently miscount objects in cluttered scenes. Reasoning models can enumerate systematically.
    • Spatial relationship extraction: "Is object A above or to the left of object B?" — reasoning models trace coordinates explicitly.
    • Chart and diagram interpretation: Reasoning models read axes, extract values, and perform calculations over visual data.
    • Multi-image comparison: With extended reasoning, models can hold details from multiple images in the chain-of-thought and compare them systematically.

    o3 and o4-mini Specifics¶

    • o3 and o4-mini support image inputs natively and apply the same extended reasoning paradigm used for text-only tasks.
    • OpenAI's benchmarks show substantial gains over GPT-4o on visual math (geometry diagrams, function plots), scientific figure interpretation, and spatial reasoning tasks.
    • The trade-off is latency and cost — reasoning over an image may take 10-30x longer than a simple perception response.

    • Qwen3-VL's thinking/non-thinking mode switch is notable: the model can choose to allocate extended reasoning only when the visual task demands it, saving compute on simple perception queries.

    Safety Surface Expansion¶

    Multimodal input fundamentally expands the attack surface for adversarial use. When a model accepts images alongside text, every pixel becomes a potential vector for manipulation.

    Prompt Injection via Images¶

    The most immediate new threat is prompt injection embedded in visual input:

    • Text-in-image injection: Adversaries embed instructions as text within screenshots, documents, or photographs. The model's OCR capability reads the injected text and may follow it as if it were part of the system prompt.
    • Covert instructions: Instructions can be rendered in low-contrast text, watermarks, or steganographic patterns that are invisible to casual human inspection but readable by the model's vision encoder.
    • Screenshot-based attacks: In agent/computer-use settings, a malicious website can render instructions on-screen that the agent reads and follows.

    System Card Multimodal Safety Sections¶

    Frontier model system cards now include explicit multimodal safety sections. These typically cover:

    • CSAM and explicit content detection in visual inputs
    • Jailbreak robustness specifically for image-based prompt injection
    • Bias in visual perception — models may exhibit different accuracy or behavior depending on demographic characteristics visible in images
    • Multimodal refusal calibration — ensuring safety refusals are neither too aggressive (refusing benign medical images) nor too permissive (allowing harmful content when instructions are split across text and image modalities)

    Cross-Modal Attack Composition¶

    A particularly challenging threat is cross-modal composition: an attack that is benign in any single modality but harmful when modalities are combined. For example, a text prompt asking "describe this process" paired with an image depicting a dangerous procedure. Models must evaluate the joint semantics, not each modality in isolation.

    Computer-Use & GUI Grounding¶

    The ability to perceive and interact with graphical user interfaces is one of the highest-value applications of multimodal LLMs. GUI grounding — mapping a natural language instruction to a specific screen coordinate or UI element — requires both high-resolution perception and spatial reasoning.

    ScreenSpot Benchmarks¶

    ScreenSpot and ScreenSpot-V2 are benchmarks that measure a model's ability to identify the correct screen location for a given instruction (e.g., "click the submit button"). Performance on ScreenSpot correlates strongly with real-world computer-use agent capability.

    • Top-performing models achieve >80% accuracy on ScreenSpot, but performance degrades significantly on non-standard UI layouts, dark mode interfaces, and small or overlapping elements.
    • Resolution matters critically here — models using dynamic resolution encoders (NaFlex) substantially outperform fixed-resolution models on small-element identification.

    Phi-Ground Dataset and Spatial Normalization¶

    Microsoft's Phi-Ground dataset provides training data specifically designed for GUI grounding:

    • Contains screen-coordinate annotations with normalized spatial coordinates (0-1000 range on each axis)
    • Trains models to output bounding boxes and click points in a standardized coordinate system
    • The normalization scheme is critical — without it, models struggle to generalize across different screen resolutions and aspect ratios

    From Perception to Action¶

    The connection between high-resolution perception and GUI agent capability is direct:

    1. Resolution: The model must resolve small UI text and icons — this requires at minimum 720p-equivalent visual token budgets.
    2. Spatial precision: The model must output coordinates accurate to within a few pixels of the target element.
    3. Temporal grounding: For multi-step tasks, the model must track UI state changes across sequential screenshots.
    4. Grounding verification: Advanced agents verify their actions by checking whether the screen changed as expected after a click or keystroke.

    Models like Phi-4-reasoning-vision and Claude's computer-use capability represent the current frontier, but accuracy on complex multi-step GUI workflows remains below human-level. The gap is primarily in temporal reasoning and recovery from unexpected UI states, not in raw perception.

    Open Challenges¶

    Text-Conditioned Image Tokenization¶

    Current vision encoders tokenize images independently of the text prompt. This means the same image produces the same tokens whether the question is about overall scene composition or a tiny detail in the corner. Text-conditioned tokenization — where the encoding adapts based on what the model needs to look for — is an active research direction that could dramatically improve token efficiency.

    Token Efficiency¶

    Visual tokens are expensive. A single 720p image consumes ~3,600 tokens — equivalent to several pages of text. For video understanding, the token cost scales linearly with frame count. Key research directions:

    • Token pruning/merging: Dynamically reducing visual token count based on information content
    • Learned downsampling: Letting the model learn to compress visual tokens after initial encoding
    • Selective frame sampling: For video, choosing which frames to encode at full resolution vs. skip or summarize

    Extreme Detail and Nuance Understanding¶

    Even frontier models struggle with:

    • Fine text in images: Small print, handwritten notes, partially occluded text
    • Subtle visual differences: Spot-the-difference tasks, minor defect detection in manufacturing images
    • Dense information displays: Complex dashboards, dense scientific figures with many overlapping elements
    • Cultural and contextual nuance: Visual humor, sarcasm conveyed through image composition, culturally specific visual references

    Optimal Reasoning/Non-Reasoning Mode Switching¶

    Models like Qwen3-VL offer both thinking (extended reasoning) and non-thinking (fast perception) modes. The open question is: when should a model engage extended reasoning for a visual task?

    • Simple object identification does not benefit from extended reasoning — it just adds latency.
    • Complex spatial reasoning, counting, and chart interpretation benefit substantially.
    • The optimal switching policy is task-dependent and currently requires either user specification or heuristic rules. Learned routing — where the model itself decides whether to "think" based on the visual input and query — is an unsolved problem with significant practical value.

    Unified Generation¶

    Most current multimodal LLMs are understanding-only — they accept images but generate only text. Unified models that both understand and generate across modalities (text, image, video, audio) remain nascent. Early examples exist (Gemini can generate images) but the quality gap between specialized generators (e.g., dedicated diffusion models) and unified multimodal LLMs remains large. Closing this gap without sacrificing understanding capability is a major open challenge.
